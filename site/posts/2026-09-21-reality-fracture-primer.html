Reality Fracture draft primer

Reality Fracture comes out on Arena on October 2. We had bots draft it 800 times (100 pods of eight). Each bot built a deck from its picks, and the decks played 100,000 games against each other under the full rules. This primer is what those games say, written as advice for your first drafts.

This run is on the complete spoiler, and for the first time every card in the set is actually simulated. Fifteen cards — thirteen of them rare or mythic — used to be missing, because the engine could not play them. Two of the fifteen turned out to be among the best uncommons in the format, so if you read an earlier version of this page, the card advice below has changed.

Each claim is tagged with how much to trust it:

  • Solid: a big effect, clear of the noise, and the kind of thing the simulator gets right on sets humans have played.
  • Lean: real in the simulation, but small or in an area where the simulator is weaker.
  • Unproven: likely to be wrong; see why.

Want a specific card or pack? The card ratings, pick helper and color pairs tools use the same data. Once the set is out, they switch to real 17Lands results by themselves.

The short version

  1. Red-green is the best pair (53.2%), and it is the only one clearly ahead. The next eight sit between 49.1% and 51.0%, so outside red-green, being in the open lane still matters more than the pair you pick. Solid.
  2. Avoid blue-black (45.7%). It is seven points behind red-green and two behind the next-worst pair. Solid.
  3. Choose to play first. The player on the play won 53.4%. Solid.
  4. Play plenty of creatures, but the effect is smaller than it looks: decks with 20 or more won 57.5% and decks with 13 or fewer won 46.4%, yet almost all of that is card quality rather than the creature count itself. Lean.
  5. Green and red are the deepest colors. White is in 58% of bot decks and wins the least of the three you would actually want to be in. Lean.
  6. Late picks worth watching for: both Garruks, Overwrite the Multiverse, Way of the Wildspeaker and Multiply by Zero. Solid.
  7. Do not first-pick Emrakul. Ten mana is ten mana. Solid.

Colors and pairs

Each pair's win rate against the whole field, with 95% intervals.

Red-green (53.2%) is alone at the top, and its interval (52.6–53.8%) does not touch the next pair's. That is a real change from the partial-spoiler version of this primer, where the top four were inseparable. Black-red (51.0%), black-green (50.8%) and blue-red (50.5%) follow, then five pairs between 49.1% and 49.9%.

Blue-black is still the pair to avoid (45.7%), and it is now further adrift than before: more than three points below the next-worst pair. Only 4% of bot decks ended up there.

By color, red (51.0%) and green (50.8%) lead, black (49.8%) and white (49.4%) follow, and blue (49.0%) trails. The number that matters for your seat is not the win rate but the traffic: 58% of bot decks played white against 30% for blue. White is the most contested color in the format by a distance, so the white cards you see late are the ones everyone else already passed.

  • Blue may be better than it looks. On The Hobbit, where real results exist, this simulator underrated blue decks. So "blue is last" here probably means "blue is playable". Blue-black's last place is the weakest call in this primer. Unproven.
  • Red-green is a creature deck and the simulator is at its best judging creature decks against each other, which is part of why it is confident here. Expect the gap to be real but smaller once people are drafting it. Lean.

What to take

Commons

The three best commons in each color by win rate when drawn.

Bestial Incursion (a 4/4 trampler for four that comes back with flashback) and Blossom-Blessed Angel (a 2/4 flier with vigilance plus a one-mana +1/+1 counter) are the best commons in the set. Take either over most uncommons.

Green runs deepest: five of the twelve best commons are green. After Bestial Incursion come Wrecking Gecko (a 5/5 ward for five), Greenhouse Propagator and Arcane Amphisbaena (a two-mana deathtouch body plus a Jace). In black, Extended Absence (exile a creature for four) and Screeching Soulbreaker are both strong. In red, Heartstring Puller (a 3/1 trampler that brings a 2/2) and Tether Technician (a 4/5 with reach that can deal 2 when it enters) are the picks.

Kiora of Fire and Ashes (six mana for a 2/2 and a 5/5 flying dragon, 61.9%), Thalia, the Survivor (a 3/4 lifelinker that taxes their spells, 60.7%) and Tetsuko Umezawa, Pursuer (a 2/4 double striker, 60.6%) lead.

Then come the two the simulator could not play until now. Ghalta the Unstoppable (59.5%) costs {8}{G} on paper, but it costs {X} less for your greatest power, so in a green deck it lands for five or six as an 8/8 trampler that gives your whole board trample. Ghalta the Immovable (57.3%) is the stranger one: a 0/7 for {8}{W}, discounted by your greatest toughness, which lets every creature you control attack as though it had no defender and assign damage by toughness instead of power. A 0/7 that attacks for seven. Both were absent from the last version of this primer — the second one was even listed as a trap — and both are now top-seven uncommons. Craftwork Crusher (58.7%) and Twisted Fates (57.5%) round out the tier, and Your Fate Ends Here (54.6%) is still the best removal spell at the rarity.

Emrakul, the Exigent Doom is the clearest trap in the set, and the bots fall for it completely: they take it with pick 1.0 on average and win 42.7% of the games they draw it. It is a 12/12 for ten mana with a plan for getting there — exile it from your hand for {3} to turn a land into a two-mana-producing land, then cast it from exile — and in a 40-card deck that plan still does not get there often enough. Take the playable card instead.

Proft, Sinister Mastermind (44.1% at pick 4.6) reads like a three-mana 5/5 menace, but you cannot cast it until your graveyard holds seven cards, which is late or never in Limited. Germinate Recruits (39.0%) does nothing unless you gained life that turn. Flickering Hound is a 2/2 for four whose trigger rarely matters. Return to the Light Realms costs seven and does not stabilise a board.

Empower Jace and the Way cycle

Empower Jace is the set's main mechanic. It puts loyalty counters on your Jace token, creating one if you don't have one. Across its 33 cards it averages 1.3 points below the set's average card — slightly negative on its own, not the free upside it looks like. What decides a card is its payload. The clearest case is the uncommon Way cycle:

Win rate when drawn for each Way, with 95% intervals. The dashed line is the set's average card.

Wildspeaker (55.2%) and Paradox (54.1%) are above average, Warlord (52.4%) and Deathbringer (52.1%) are just above, and Healer sits on it. Cryomancer, Mentor, Pyromancer and Necromancer are all below. Way of the Mind Sculptor looks worst of all at 31.7%, but its loyalty-removal draw is left out in the simulator, so don't read much into that one.

Two other patterns: flying creatures average +2.2 points over the set's average card, and surveil cards average −1.4 — which, like the blue result, is somewhere the simulator is known to be weak. Cards with prepare (creatures that carry a spell you can cast from them) sit at about average.

Building your deck

We compared the 800 decks' records against what was in them. We also held each deck's average card quality fixed, so "more creatures" doesn't just mean "better cards".

Deck win rate by number of creatures, with 95% intervals.

The raw trend is dramatic: decks with 20 or more creatures won 57.5%, decks with 16–17 won 51.6%, and decks with 13 or fewer won 46.4%. The average bot deck ran 15.

But hold card quality fixed and most of that disappears. Each extra creature is worth +0.26 points (±0.21) once you account for the deck's average card quality — a much smaller effect than the buckets suggest, and only barely clear of zero. This is a change from the previous version of this primer, which put it at +0.65, and the reason is that the deckbuilder got better at valuing cards: decks that used to be creature-light were also card-poor, and that confound is now smaller.

  • Removal is not measurable here. +0.07 points per removal spell (±0.28), quality held fixed. The earlier +0.6 does not survive. That does not mean removal is bad — it means in this simulation good removal spells are just good cards, and the quality term is already counting them. Lean.
  • Two-drops don't matter by themselves, same as before: −0.05 (±0.24). Don't take a weak two-drop over a strong four-drop to fix your curve. Lean.
  • Choose to play first: 53.4% on the play (±0.3). Solid.

So: take the better cards. The old advice to force 16+ creatures is weaker than it looked — what those decks really had was better cards.

The caveat from last time still stands: on The Hobbit, the simulator overrated decks full of big-bodied creatures, because bots don't punish a board of large creatures with tricks and evasion the way people do.

What the simulator can't tell you (yet)

  • The whole set is in now, but 108 of the 285 cards are still simplified. Every card is playable and simulated; 108 have some piece of text the engine leaves out, marked † in the tools with the missing part on hover. A simplification only ever makes a card weaker, so treat those numbers as floors. Ferocity of the Hunt is the clearest case: without its return-from-death half, it's just a deathtouch aura.
  • The bots' pick order is worse than their play. Emrakul is the proof: they take a ten-mana creature first pick, every time. Read a card's average pick as "what a card-counting bot thinks", not as what the pick should be, and be most suspicious of it on expensive cards.
  • Combat tricks look terrible here (Charge the Sanctum and Academic Ascent are near the bottom of the commons). Bots use tricks worse than people, so these are underrated. Don't write them off.
  • Blue and surveil are where the simulator has been wrong before. On The Hobbit it underrated both blue decks and instants. Discount the blue-black result accordingly.
  • Nothing here has met a real draft deck. The bots have never played an Arena deck from this set, because none exist yet.

How this updates

The tools refresh on their own. Once a day, they fetch Reality Fracture's live 17Lands data. When real players have enough games with a card, its real win rate replaces the simulated one, and the tools say which numbers are real. After release, we'll check this primer against 17Lands in a new post.
